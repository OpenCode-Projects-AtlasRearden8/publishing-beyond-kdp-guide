# Getting into libraries

Libraries are the quiet, patient customers of publishing. One library copy of
an ebook can be borrowed dozens of times; a print copy circulates for years.
And most indie authors have never tried the doors — because the doors are
not on KDP's dashboard. Here are the four that matter.

## Door 1: Library aggregators (the easiest yes)

**Draft2Digital** (which absorbed Findaway Voices and, earlier, Smashwords)
offers library distribution to **OverDrive** — the biggest ebook platform that
libraries use (the app most patrons know is
**Libby**). As of this guide, D2D takes a standard ebook-aggregator cut
(roughly 10% off the list price) and pushes your title wherever OverDrive
systems accept indie pricing.

Round numbers for a library ebook sale: libraries typically buy "One Copy /
One User" (OC/OU) licenses at **$30–70** — several times the $9.99 consumer
price — or "Cost Per Circulation" (CPC) models where the library pays a
smaller fee each time the book is borrowed, usually a few dollars, up to a
cap. Either way, each library sale is worth more than several consumer
sales, and your royalty is the usual aggregator share of the license price.

Setup: publish wide through D2D, tick the library distribution option,
upload, done. This is a single checkbox for most titles.

## Door 2: The Indie Author Project (free, curated, nationwide)

The **Indie Author Project** (run with Library Journal and BiblioBoard) is a
juried program: you submit your ebook free, curators review it, and selected
titles are offered to public libraries across the US and Canada through
BiblioBoard with **no cost to the library and a royalty to you per use**.
Winning or being featured also puts your book in front of acquisitions
librarians during a slower news cycle. Search "Indie Author Project" for the
current submission page — it is free.

Round numbers: **$0 to submit**, royalty per circulation in the **cents to
low dollars** range, but the *reach* is real — hundreds of systems — and
"Library Journal featured indie" is a line you can put on an author bio.

## Door 3: Palace Marketplace (author-set pricing)

**Palace Marketplace** (from LibraryPass, the team behind the Palace reading
app used by public libraries) lets indie authors set pricing on ebooks for
library distribution, often at prices the author sets (frequently in the
**$10–50** range per license, similar to OverDrive's OC/OU). Libraries using
the Palace app can buy directly from the marketplace. This is the newest of
the doors and the one where the author has the most pricing control.

## Door 4: The library *print* copy (through Ingram)

Print books enter libraries the same way they enter bookstores: a librarian
orders from **Ingram** (or Baker & Taylor) if your title is in the catalog
price, returnable policy, and BISAC category line up. That is the
[wide distribution](03-wide-distribution.md) checklist doing quiet work:

- A **real ISBN with a real imprint** (not "Independently published") helps
  acquisitions systems trust the record.
- **Returnable** titles get ordered more; non-returnable ones often get a
  second look. Some library systems only order returnable inventory.
- **BISAC subjects** (e.g., "COMPUTERS / Artificial Intelligence / General")
  decide which librarians find the book. Choose subjects the way you would
  choose a genre page, not the way you would choose a homework assignment.

A short, polite email to the **acquisitions or collection-development
librarian** of your own local system — "I am a local author; my book is
X, published [date], returnable through Ingram at 40% for $16.99; I would
love it if the library considered it" — works surprisingly often, because
"local author" is exactly the kind of book librarians are proud to pull.

## What the doors don't do

- Libraries will not sell your book widely to *everyone* who wants a copy of
  your guide — that is not their job. Circulation moves in tens of reads
  per year per system, which is exactly right for a reference guide that
  stays useful.
- Getting into 300 library *systems* is not the same as reaching 30,000
  readers. Treat library income as steady-background math, not a
  breakthrough.

## Costs and royalties, round numbers

| Door | Costs you | Royalty shape | Payoff speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| D2D → OverDrive | $0 setup, aggregator cut from list | % of a $30–70 OC/OU license (or CPC fees) | Months |
| Indie Author Project | $0 | Per-circulation royalty (cents to a few $) | Slow; the marketing isn't immediate |
| Palace Marketplace | $0 | % of the license price you set | Months |
| Ingram print | IngramSpark setup (about $49–69 per format) | List − wholesale discount − print | Library orders trickle like bookstore orders |

> **The underrated trick:** a title in 300 library systems keeps being
> borrowed by readers who *would never have seen an ad*. Because library
> use is measured in years, a reference book like *The Massive AI Manual*
> keeps earning through whole editions of new patrons. That is the whole
> reason to spend the afternoon on this chapter and then go back to
> writing.
