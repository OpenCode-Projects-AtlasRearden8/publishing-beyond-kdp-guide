# Author taxes in plain numbers

Plain copy: you publish a book, revenue shows up, and the tax-man intersection
is not complicated — it is just *unfamiliar*: you are now a tiny business
with a Schedule C. This chapter gives the round-number version, all US
federal unless noted. **It is education, not tax advice** — for filing, use a
CPA or trusted software, and confirm numbers for your exact year.

## The 12,000-foot view

You earn money from book sales in one of three places:

1. **As a sole proprietor** — a Schedule C inside your personal tax return
   (the default for everyone).
2. **Through an LLC** — exactly the same Schedule C for a single-member LLC
   that hasn't elected otherwise. The LLC changes paperwork *with your
   state*, and turns "you owe personally" into "the LLC owes" — taxes
   are still yours and still Schedule C.
3. **S-corp election** — not even on your radar until consistent profit
   clears high five figures; a CPA conversation, not a DIY project.

Every platform's dashboard knows you exist and will 1099 you for your year.
Your job in one sentence: **keep receipts, keep a log of income, subtract
business expenses from income (profit), and pay tax on the profit.**

## Self-employment tax: the one number nobody tells you about

On top of income tax, freelancers and authors pay **self-employment (SE)
tax** — the worker-AND-employer halves of Social Security + Medicare that
employees split with their boss. Employee-side people see this on a pay stub
as "FICA." You now pay both halves: **15.3%**.

Two adjustments make the number less painful:

- It applies to **92.35%** of net profit (the IRS discounts a sliver equal
  to the employer-half you would have "not had" as an employee).
- You can **deduct half of the SE tax** from your taxable income, and deduct
  ordinary business expenses from net profit first.

Plain-arithmetic example — **$10,000 of net profit** (sales minus expenses):

| Line | Math | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| SE tax: 92.35% × $10,000 × 15.3% | 9235 × 0.153 | **$1,413** |
| Half of SE tax deducted from income | $1,413 ÷ 2 | −$706 |
| Income-tax base | $10,000 − $706 (and standard deduction etc.) | $9,294 |
| Income tax if 12% bracket applies to most of it | ~$929 × 0.12 | ~$1,115 |
| Rough effective combined tax on $10,000 | | **~ 25%, or about $2,500** |

## The same math at other income levels (single filer brackets, round)

| Net profit | SE tax | Rough income tax | Rough total | Rough % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $5,000 | $706 | ~ $150 | ~ $860 | ~ 17% |
| $10,000 | $1,413 | ~ $1,100 | ~ $2,500 | ~ 25% |
| $25,000 | $3,534 | ~ $3,300 | ~ $6,800 | ~ 27% |
| $50,000 | $7,068 | ~ $6,900 | ~ $14,000 | ~ 28% |

These are deliberately rough (single, standard deduction, no kids' credits,
all-bracket-sweep shortcuts). The shape is what matters: **the first dollars
of profit are taxed lightest; the percentage stops moving much around the
mid-twenties of income.**

For *state* tax, Indiana (where Asdale Dynamics, LLC is registered) has a
flat rate around **3%**, plus county option taxes in some counties — so add
roughly $300–500 per $10,000 of net profit in planning terms.

> A quick rule of thumb that works until it doesn't: **hold back 30¢ of
> every profit dollar** for federal + state + SE tax, and you will almost
> never be surprised.

you skip (28¢–30¢ held out of a taxed profit). The list includes:

## Quarterly payments — the part that surprises year-one authors

If you will owe **$1,000 or more at tax time**, the IRS expects you to pay
*estimated* tax during the year, quarterly:

| Quarter | Covers income | Due |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jan–Mar | April 15 |
| 2 | Apr–May | June 15 |
| 3 | Jun–Aug | Sept 15 |
| 4 | Sep–Dec | January 15 (next year) |

Round-number shortcut without accounting software: compute your expected
year's tax on the tables above, divide by four, send that amount each
quarter through the IRS Direct Pay site or your state portal. Miss the
quarters and the penalty is small-but-not-zero (a few percent of the
underpayment).

## Sales tax on direct sales — the one real complexity

Book sales on Amazon/Ingram are handled by the *platform* (marketplace
facilitator rules). Direct sales are *yours*. Rules vary by state:

- **Physical copies shipped to a buyer**: in most states, selling your own
  books shipped to that state triggers sales-tax collection. Registering in
  your own state (Indiana here) and collecting there is the distance you must
  cross first.
- **"Economic nexus"**: you only owe sales tax in a state once your sales
  there pass a threshold — commonly **$100,000 revenue or 200 transactions
  per year** in that state. A first-time indie author will almost certainly
  be under that everywhere.
- **Digital products** (ebooks): about half the states tax them. If your
  direct-sales platform handles tax for you (Shopify with the right tax
  app, Lemon Squeezy — "merchant of record" behavior), the problem shrinks
  to filing.

Plain-language plan for a first-time direct seller:

1. Register in your home state once volume is real (a few hundred sales).
2. Use a platform that calculates and remits for you where possible.
3. Keep a log of direct sales by state, and revisit once a year.

## The LLC-shaped footnote

You already have the LLC (Asdale Dynamics, LLC, Indiana). Doing publishing
through it is sensible the way bookkeeping through a shoebox is not:

- Keep book income (**royalties, direct-sales**) flowing to a dedicated
  business bank account.
- An **EIN** (free from the IRS site in ten minutes) lets you avoid giving
  your SSN to platforms that accept it.
- Indiana's LLC annual report is cheap and due in the registration
  anniversary window — calendar it once.
- Business income still lands on your personal Schedule C (single-member
  LLC, no S-corp election) — nothing about taxes above changes.

Round-number first-year mental budget for a *wide* launch (per title):
about **$1,000–1,500** of production costs (cover, editing, ISBNs,
IngramSpark setup) — almost all deductible, and sitting at roughly
**$170–300 of tax offset** in the SE-tax/income overlap arithmetic above.
That is what "deductible" really means: the business bought something;
the tax bill shrinks a bit; nobody hands you a refund check for it.
